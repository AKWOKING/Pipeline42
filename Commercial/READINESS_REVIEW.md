## Evaluation notes

The six scores are evidence-based judgments against the supplied 0–5 rubric, in this order: **A** Problem Proximity, **B** Active Outbound / Prospecting Evidence, **C** Data / Workflow Ownership, **D** Observable Problem Signal, **E** Buyer Accessibility, and **F** Purchasing Authority / Budget Influence. B scores require public evidence of outbound activity; a sales or Sales Development title alone does not establish an active outbound motion. A low B score can coexist with high problem proximity or workflow ownership. For F, 0 means **not publicly evidenced**, not that the person lacks authority: none of the sources reviewed verifies a specific prospect's budget ownership or purchase authority for data/validation services, and titles alone are not used as budget evidence. Actual budget, prior spend, and willingness remain unknown. The total is a simple `/30` descriptive sum—not a buying-likelihood, pain, willingness-to-pay, or payment score. Selection is deliberately not the ten highest totals.

**Status meanings:** `OUTREACH-READY` means current role/company/workflow have enough public support for a neutral discovery opener; it does not mean qualified pain or a buyer. `VERIFY FIRST` means a material role, company, or current-workflow fact remains unclear. `HOLD` means current relevance is absent or too weak for this experiment. `UNVERIFIED` means a fact is unresolved or conflicting; it is not proof that the fact is false.

**Problem-signal discipline:** a D score of 1 means only generic workflow relevance, not a reported problem. A D score of 0 means no current relevant problem signal was found; it does not mean the problem is absent. Nick Abraham's D=4 is a historical self-reported issue in an Ocean.io-hosted case study, and the same story describes adopting Ocean.io; it may already be solved and is not independently verified. Jen Igartua's D=4 is Go Nimbly's public report of client requests for data cleanup/enrichment, not evidence that Jen herself needs Pipeline42. No prospect has publicly established an unresolved personal problem, a current need for this audit, or willingness to pay.

The tracker’s U.S. geography is still a **provisional assumption**, not a validated ICP boundary. A public U.S. location for some people does not validate that commercial scope.

## SECTION 3 — Final 10

### Shared qualification checklist (apply to every selected person)

These are questions to discover, not facts. Ask conversationally; do not deliver this as a ten-question interrogation:

1. Does a sales/prospect-data problem actually occur?
2. How often, and how many records or actions are affected?
3. What happens when it gets through to a rep or campaign?
4. Who catches it and who fixes it?
5. How is it solved today?
6. What time, people, tools, or other resources does that solution consume?
7. Has the company paid someone specifically to solve it? How much/result?
8. Who owns or approves the budget?
9. Would they provide a real, permitted 10-record list for a test?
10. After a concrete problem and consequence are established, would they pay **$150** for a **10-Record Sales Data Audit, 24-hour turnaround**?

Do not offer on the basis of a hypothetical complaint. The offer gate is a specific recent example, evidence of recurrence or consequence, a workflow owner, and permission to inspect records. Ask; let the person decide. A “yes” to interest or willingness is not payment.

**Access status for all ten:** a public professional route is available through the profile/company links in the tracker, but message delivery/readability and response are unknown. Access to a real 10-record sample, data-sharing permission, and authority to approve a test are also unknown for every prospect.
